## Section 1: The Hierarchy

Sense-line breaks are governed by four criteria, applied in a strict hierarchy. This is the harmonized formulation, established 2026-04-11 and reframed 2026-04-16 with the "default + unless" mechanism.

### The Four Criteria

**Criterion 1: Atomic Thought — PRIMARY, floor.**

Each line should normally contain one complete idea unit.

By default, this means one complete predication — a unit of meaning that can stand on its own. However, predication is the default operationalization, not the criterion itself.

The criterion is Stan's attempt to further flesh out Skousen's proposal of a "sense-line" concept (Royal Skousen, *The Earliest Text*, 2009/2022) through iterative experimentation with the texts, including determining preliminary criteria for how to identify sense-line boundaries, testing line breaks, reading aloud, and then examining and refining what "one unit of meaning" actually means in practice.

This work intriguingly converged with Chafe's "idea unit" (one focus of consciousness), which was identified afterward as an opportunity for further theoretical grounding beyond Skousen that cohered with Stan's experiments. See Section 7 for the full genealogy.

When a line fails the initial predication test, check the two-prong exception test (Section 2) before merging. The exception test prevents the predication default from being narrower than the cognitive foundation warrants.

Atomic thought should be the default when there are collisions. However, the "unless" mechanism (Section 2) prevents this from being a blank check for merging everything into predication-sized chunks.

**Criterion 2: Single Image — STRONG, ceiling. Split signal.**

Each line should paint a single image or picture in the mind. If a line contains two distinct images, it is a candidate for splitting. If a line contains no complete image, it may need merging with its neighbor.

The image test sharpens the atomic thought test by asking: "what does the reader SEE here?" Close your eyes and picture the scene. Does the line make you see one thing? The image test operates within syntactic permission — it never overrides a syntactic bond.

**Criterion 3: Breath Unit — SECONDARY, tiebreaker within the valid range.**

Each line can be delivered in one breath at natural reading pace. This is the oral-delivery criterion, central to the BOM Reader's design for ESL readers, children, and newcomers.

Very short fragments (1-2 words) are rarely valid unless they are complete predications (imperatives, vocatives-as-commands, classical commata). Very long lines likely contain multiple thoughts.

Honest about its current operational limitations: no falsifiable breath-length threshold has been defined. The breath test currently functions as a sanity check — if a merge produces something unspeakable, reconsider — but not as a mechanical gate. Stan: "The breath unit should give us pause and ask if there IS another way to consider the text, because people generally don't write/speak more than a coherent breath unit."

**Criterion 4: Source-Language Syntax — SERVANT.**

Line breaks are informed by the clause and syntax structure of the source language — for the BOM Reader, English clause structure (with awareness of underlying Hebrew patterns where relevant). Conditional constructions, subordinating conjunctions, participial phrases, discourse markers, and verb-complement bonds all signal natural break points.

This criterion serves the first three — it does not override them. Grammar helps us find where atomic thoughts, images, and breath units naturally break. **If a grammatical rule produces a line that isn't an atomic thought, the rule is wrong, not the criteria.** Grammar reveals structure that already exists in the text; it does not create structure.

### The Hierarchy in Practice

```
Criterion 1 (atomic thought)  — primary, floor. Default = predication; unless = two-prong test.
Criterion 2 (single image)    — strong, ceiling. Split signal.
Criterion 3 (breath unit)     — secondary, tiebreaker when thought/image are ambiguous.
Criterion 4 (syntax)          — servant. Reveals structure, doesn't create it.
```

### The Cognitive Cycle

The theoretical grounding for the entire methodology is the cognitive cycle:

**The author thinks** (idea units in consciousness) **-> writes** (encodes into syntax) **-> the reader hears/reads** (decodes from syntax) **-> thinks** (reconstructs idea units in consciousness).

Predication is the most common encoding of idea units, but it is not the only encoding. The two-prong exception test (Section 2) asks: (1) is there an idea unit here? and (2) can the reader reconstruct it from the formal structure? When both answers are yes, the line is valid — the cognitive cycle completes even without explicit predication.

### The Priority Order When Criteria Leave Ambiguity

**Chunking > Oral > Rhetorical**

When the four criteria leave room for multiple valid choices:

1. **Cognitive chunking** — line breaks first serve comprehension. Each line is a unit the reader can process as one cognitive bite. This is the foundational purpose of colometric formatting.
2. **Oral delivery** — line breaks support read-aloud at natural breath pace. This is the ESL/youth/audio purpose.
3. **Rhetorical structure revelation** — line breaks make the author's compositional architecture visible (parallels, escalation, climax, chiasm). This is the literary/scholarly purpose.

When these conflict, chunking wins. A break that aids cognitive chunking but flattens some rhetoric is acceptable. A break that reveals rhetoric but creates a fragment that cannot be processed is not.

### The Mission

**We expose atomic thoughts, not poetic parallels.** Stan:

> "Parallels are fine, but we were trying to isolate clear ideas, not half strophes or poetic structure, except indirectly."

> "Atomic thoughts trump rhetorical considerations. Our effort here is to expose SENSE-lines, not poetic parallels. While we might overlap with Parry, we're not trying to 'do Parry.'"

The editorial goal is to expose discrete units of thought that an ESL reader, child, or newcomer can process one bite at a time. Hebrew/Greek poetic structure is incidental to that goal. When the two align, that is a happy bonus. When they conflict, atomic thought wins.

**Rhetorical force ≠ separate thought-line unit.** A phrase can be rhetorically powerful without being a separate thought. The polysyndetic triple "to a wild and a hardened and a ferocious people" has rhetorical force but is NOT a separate thought — it is the indirect object of "to preach." It merges. The test is always criterion 1 (does this line carry its own thought?), never "does this line sound powerful?" This is a recurring trench-Claude failure mode: mistaking emotional or rhetorical weight for structural independence.

## Section 2: The Unless Conditions (Closed List)

When a line fails the strict predication test, it may still stand if it passes BOTH prongs of the exception test. If either prong fails, merge. The two-prong requirement prevents the exception from becoming a blank check — editorial intuition alone cannot override the predication default.

### The Two-Prong Exception Test

**Cognitive prong:** Is this a demonstrable Chafe idea unit — one new focus of consciousness the reader processes as a single chunk?

**Structural prong:** Is there a formal justification for the reader to reconstruct the full thought?

Both must pass. The cognitive prong without the structural prong is just "it feels like an idea unit" — not enough. The structural prong without the cognitive prong is just "there is a grammatical pattern" — not enough either.

### The Four Structural Justifications (Closed List)

**Generating principle:** All four justifications are instances of a single underlying property: *formal structure in the text enables cognitive recovery of the full predication despite its absence from the line.* The reader can reconstruct "who did what" because the text provides machinery — parallel markers, accumulation patterns, speech-act conventions — that makes the missing predicate recoverable. If no such machinery exists, the line is genuinely incomplete and must merge.

This list is extensible only by worked examples + adversarial validation. Any proposed fifth justification must (a) demonstrate that it is a genuinely distinct instance of the generating principle, not a restatement of an existing justification, and (b) survive an adversarial challenge.

#### 1. Formally-Marked Parallel Series

**Definition:** Members of a series connected by formal markers ("and also," "nor," correlative particles, polysyndetic "and") where the shared predicate is cognitively recoverable from the parallel structure.

**Cognitive prong argument:** The reader does not experience each member as incomplete; they experience it as a beat in a series whose predicate is active in working memory. Each "and [participle]" in a polysyndetic series reactivates the shared auxiliary.

**Structural prong argument:** The formal markers (repeated conjunction, elided auxiliary, parallel grammatical structure) provide the reader with the machinery to reconstruct the full predication for each member.

**PASS example — Alma 21:9:** "concerning the coming of Christ, / and also concerning the resurrection of the dead" — each is a topic-frame under the governing verb "began to open the scriptures unto them." Cognitive: each is a distinct idea unit (a different theological topic Aaron taught). Structural: formally-marked parallel with "and also concerning" signaling shared predicate. Both prongs pass.

**PASS example — polysyndetic series:** "and scourged, / and cast out, / and disowned" — each participle is an implied predication with elided auxiliary. Cognitive: each is a distinct idea unit (a different kind of suffering). Structural: polysyndetic series with elided auxiliary. Both prongs pass.

**FAIL example:** "and the atonement of his blood" — a bare coordinate noun phrase. Cognitive: this is part of a larger theological claim, not a distinct focus of consciousness. Structural: no formal parallel marker that enables predicate recovery; this is a compound object of a governing verb, not a member of an independent series. Neither prong passes; merge with the governing clause.

#### 2. Portrait-Building Attribute Accumulation

**Definition:** A stack of attributes painting a single portrait where the image, not the predicate, is the structural unit.

**Cognitive prong argument:** A set of attributes building one portrait functions as one cognitive chunk even without an explicit verb, because the portrait IS the idea unit.

**Structural prong argument:** The accumulation pattern (adjective, adjective, prepositional phrase) is a recognized grammatical form for predicate-complement stacking. The reader reconstructs "X is [attribute]" or "X who is [attribute]" from context.

**PASS example:** "full of grace and mercy and truth" — portrait of Christ's nature. Cognitive: one focus of consciousness (what Christ is like). Structural: predicate-adjective stacking pattern with shared copula recoverable from context.

**FAIL example:** A bare noun list without portrait-building function — "gold and silver and precious things" — is not a portrait; it is a catalogue. No cognitive unity beyond "stuff they had." The image test also fails: you cannot visualize "gold and silver and precious things" as a single mental picture distinct from the preceding action. Merge with governing clause.

#### 3. Speech-Act Announcement Convention

**Definition (refined 2026-04-17, pruned 2026-04-18):** Formulas that announce a direct speech act where the announcement itself is a complete communicative predication and the content follows as quoted speech. The announcement and quoted content are cognitively separate frames — one is the narrator's attribution, the other is the character's voice.

**Two canonical cases:**
1. **Direct discourse with colon/voice shift:** *"And the voice of the Lord came to Ammon, saying:"* / *"Thou shalt not go up to the land of Nephi..."* — the colon marks the shift from narrator to character voice.
2. **Formulaic prophetic announcement:** *"Thus saith the Lord God, who is the Almighty:"* / *"Except ye repent ye shall be smitten..."* — the formula is a recognized direct-speech introducer, often with colon.

**Cognitive prong argument:** The speech act IS the predication. The reader processes the announcement as a complete communicative event; the quoted content is a separate cognitive frame in a different voice.

**Structural prong argument:** The colon or other voice-shift marker provides the formal machinery licensing the boundary.

**Exclusion — IMPORTANT:** Indirect discourse (speech verb + "that" clause, no voice shift) is NOT a speech-act announcement. It is an integrated indirect-discourse proposition governed by Rule 17 (Complement Integrity).

**Parallel "that" series after a speech verb** — previously listed as sub-type #3 here — was **PRUNED 2026-04-18**. That case belongs entirely to Rule 17's exception list (frame merges with first "that" clause; subsequent parallel "that" clauses stack as Formally-Marked Parallel Series per Justification #1). It was exposition-redundant to repeat Rule 17's treatment here. See Rule 17's exception list for the parallel-series handling.

**PASS example:** *"And the voice of the Lord came to Ammon, saying: / Thou shalt not go up to the land of Nephi..."* — voice shift with colon.
**FAIL example (single-complement indirect):** *"And he did also declare unto them / that they were a people who were under him"* — indirect discourse (Rule 17 merge), not speech-act announcement.

#### 4. Classical Comma

**Definition:** A very short emphatic unit with disproportionate semantic weight. Named after Marschall's term from Pseudo-Demetrius and Cicero (the classical "komma" — a short member of a period).

**Cognitive prong argument:** These carry enough force to constitute a cognitive chunk despite lacking traditional predication structure. "Yea." or "If not so," — the reader processes each as a single, complete communicative beat.

**Structural prong argument:** The brevity itself is the structural justification. A one-word or two-word fragment that the author placed in isolation is doing rhetorical work that a longer elaboration would dilute. The author's choice to compress is formally visible.

**PASS example:** "Yea." (1 Nephi 14:8) — standalone affirmation carrying full communicative weight. Cognitive: complete idea unit (emphatic agreement/confirmation). Structural: brevity + isolation = deliberate emphasis.

**FAIL example:** "and also" standing alone — too light to carry communicative weight; it is a conjunction fragment, not a compressed idea. Merge with the clause it connects.

## Section 3: The Rules

### Autonomy Boundary (Read This First)

When proposing sense-line changes, classify each change:

- **Category A — Editorial slippage:** suboptimal break, no theological or rhetorical stakes. Apply confidently.
- **Category B — Rhetorical shape:** changing the break changes how the speaker builds an argument. Flag and ask Stan before applying.
- **Category C — Theological weight:** break placement makes a doctrinal point. Flag and discuss with Stan before touching.

**Default:** When you cannot confidently assign Category A, treat it as Category B and flag for Stan. The cost of a false Category A (applying a change Stan would have wanted to discuss) is higher than the cost of a false Category B (flagging something that turns out to be straightforward).

### Mechanical Rules (reproducible, falsifiable)

**Rule 1: AICTP Formula Integrity.**
"And it came to pass" is an indivisible discourse marker that opens a Front-End Frame (FEF). Never break the formula mid-phrase. Applied to all variants: "And it came to pass," "And now it came to pass," "And it shall come to pass," etc.
*Serves:* Criterion 1 (atomic thought) and Criterion 4 (syntax).
*Example:* "And it came to pass that in the seventh year of the reign of the judges, / there were about three thousand five hundred souls..."
*Note:* "That" belongs with the clause it introduces (Rule 16), not trailing the formula. See Section 5 (FEF Framework) for the full treatment.

**Rule 2 — CULLED 2026-04-18:** "It is expedient that" was previously its own rule; it is now a named example under **Rule 18 (Fixed Idiom Integrity)**. No independent rule logic — every "it is expedient that" case fires Rule 18's "indivisible fixed idiom" test. See Rule 18.

**Rule 3 — CULLED 2026-04-18 (FUZZY revisit).** Fully redundant with Rule 15 (Vocative Units Indivisible) + Rule 8 (Framing Devices Attach). The canonical example "And now, O king, / what great evil hast thou done" (Mosiah 12:13) is already protected by: Rule 15 keeping "O king" intact (multi-word vocative indivisibility), and Rule 8 keeping "And now" attached to its content. Rule 3 added no independent diagnostic test; its "rhetorical address formula" classification was just pattern-recognition on structures Rule 15 + Rule 8 already handle. Delete-test passes: remove Rule 3, and the Mosiah 12:13 treatment is preserved by Rule 15 + Rule 8 independently.

**Rule 5: Equivalence Restatement ("Or" as Appositive).**
When "or" introduces a restatement of the prior clause — not a genuine alternative but a renaming or specification of it — keep it with the material it restates.
*Serves:* Criterion 1 (atomic thought).
*Diagnostic (sharpened 2026-04-18):* Can "that is to say" or "in other words" replace "or" without changing the meaning? If yes, it is equivalence-"or" — merge. If "or" names a genuinely different outcome or option, it is disjunction-"or" — keep separated.
*Example:* "and they have a part in the first resurrection, / or have eternal life, being redeemed by the Lord" (Mosiah 15:24) — "or have eternal life" restates/specifies "the first resurrection." Paraphrase test: "that is to say, have eternal life" works.
*Contrast (disjunction-or):* "that he may live or die" — genuine alternative, not restatement. Keep separated.
*Note (FUZZY revisit 2026-04-18):* Rule 5 does independent work that Criterion 1 alone cannot replicate. Both equivalence-"or" and disjunction-"or" clauses can individually pass Criterion 1 as atomic thoughts; what varies is whether they are the SAME thought in different words (merge) or two distinct thoughts (separated). The paraphrase diagnostic sharpens the previously-imprecise "restatement vs. alternative" classification.

**Rule 6: Causal Clauses Break.**
"Because" introduces a new line. Causal clauses are new explanatory frames.
*Serves:* Criterion 1 (atomic thought) and Criterion 2 (new image).
*Example:* "they did murmur against their father / because he had brought them out of the land."
*Exception:* Short-line contexts where the combined line passes both the atomic thought and breath tests may merge.

**Rule 7: Purpose Clauses Break.**
"That they might / that ye may" introduces a new line when the preceding line is already a complete atomic thought. Purpose clauses are new telic frames.
*Serves:* Criterion 1 (atomic thought) and Criterion 2 (new image).
*Example:* "he went forth among the people / that he might preach the word of God unto them."
*Exception:* Short-line contexts where the combined line passes both tests may merge. The earlier formulation overpromised by treating the break as absolute.

**Rule 9: Never End a Line on a Conjunction.**
"And," "or," "but," "nor" dangling at line end is always wrong. Move the conjunction to lead the next line.
*Serves:* Criterion 4 (syntax).
*No exceptions.*

**Rule 11: Never End a Line on an Article.**
"The," "a," "an" at end of line is always wrong. Merge forward.
*Serves:* Criterion 4 (syntax).
*No exceptions.*

**Rule 12: Never Split Auxiliary from Main Verb.**
"Did / slay" is always wrong. Merge to "did slay."
*Serves:* Criterion 4 (syntax).
*No exceptions.*

**Rule 13a: Never End a Line on a Preposition Seeking Its Object.**
"The power / of the Lamb" is always wrong. Merge forward.
*Serves:* Criterion 4 (syntax).
*Exceptions:* Phrasal verb particles ("give / up" is acceptable if "up" is a particle, not a preposition) and stranded prepositions in relative clauses ("the things which they spake / of" may be acceptable when the relative clause is the line unit).

**Rule 13b: Never Split Negation from What It Negates.**
"Not / the power of God" is always wrong. Merge forward. Applies to "not," "neither," "nor," "no."
*Serves:* Criterion 4 (syntax).
*No exceptions.*

**Rule 15: Vocative Units Are Indivisible.**
Multi-word divine address formulas ("O Lord God," "O Lord our God,") stay whole on one line. The vocative can stand as its own line, but it must not be split mid-address.
*Serves:* Criterion 1 (atomic thought).
*Example:* "O Lord God," / "how long wilt thou suffer..." is correct. "O Lord" / "God" is always wrong.

**Rule 16: "Dangling That" After AICTP.**
Break BEFORE "that" so it leads the next line. "And it shall come to pass" / "that [content]." The subordinator "that" introduces a clause and must lead, not trail.
*Serves:* Criterion 4 (syntax).
*Example:* "And it shall come to pass / that whosoever shall believe on the Son of God..."
*Note:* This was the single most common mechanical error across the corpus (~25-30 instances).

**Rule 17: Complement Integrity.**
A governing verb keeps its clausal complement on the same line when the verb's predication is semantically incomplete without that complement. The verb and its complement form one integrated thought; breaking between them produces a first line that fails Criterion 1 (atomic thought). This principle covers several verb classes:
- **Causative verbs** + "that" clause: *caused that, suffered that, permitted that, commanded that, granted that*
- **Aspectual verbs** + infinitive/gerund: *began to, ceased to, continued to*
- **Speech verbs in indirect discourse** + "that" complement: *said that, declared that, testified that, swore that, spake that, proclaimed that, told that, confessed that, rehearsed that* (settled 2026-04-17, commit `b04cae9`, 38 merges corpus-wide)
- **FEF extraposition** + infinitive: *it was their lot to have fallen, it is expedient to do X* — the extraposition frame requires its infinitive complement

*Serves:* Criterion 1 (atomic thought) and Criterion 4 (syntax). Each of these verb classes is semantically dependent on its complement — the verb alone doesn't carry a complete predication.

*Exceptions (keep split):*
- **Direct discourse** — colon or "saying:" after the speech verb signals voice shift to quoted speech. Frame and quoted content are separate voices.
- **AICTP** — Rule 16 requires break before "that" in the AICTP formula.
- **Purpose "that"** — Rule 7 keeps purpose clauses ("that they might," "that ye may") on their own line.
- **Formally-marked parallel "that" series** (3+ coordinate complements under one speech verb) — merge frame with FIRST "that" clause; stack subsequent parallels.
- **Meta-announcement constructions** — BE-verb (copula) + predicate noun + appositive "that" clause. The "that" clause is appositive to the predicate noun of the copular construction, not a complement of a speech verb. *Diagnostic: does the main verb conjugate as a form of BE?* Example: *"this IS the word which I declare unto you, that many of you have begun to search..."* — DNM.
- **Direct divine speech with recitativum "that"** — *"saith the Lord, that [first-person content]"* — functionally equivalent to direct quotation with "that" as a recitativum marker; keep split.

*Example (causative):* "He caused that his servants should stand forth" — merge.
*Example (speech, indirect):* "I say unto you that the time shall come" — merge.
*Example (FEF):* "And, as it happened, it was their lot to have fallen into the hands of..." — merge. "As it happened" is an AICTP variant; scanners must not assume "it came to pass" is the only surface form.
*Example (parallel series, frame+first):* "And he did also declare unto them that they were a people who were under him, / and that they were a free people, / that they were free from the oppressions..." — frame with first, stack rest.

*Diagnostic when in doubt:* Delete the intervening noun phrase (if any). If the sentence still works as "[subject] [verb] that X," the "that" clause is a complement — merge. If deletion breaks the sentence, the "that" clause is appositive to a noun — DNM. Applied to "I spake concerning the convincing of the Jews, that Jesus is the very Christ": delete "concerning the convincing of the Jews" → "I spake that Jesus is the very Christ" works → merge.

*Precedence with Rule 19:* Rule 17 trumps Rule 19 when both apply. A "that" clause that is both cataphoric (Rule 19: break) AND the complement of a Rule 17 governing verb (merge) gets MERGED — complement integrity wins. Rule 19 governs cataphoric "that" clauses in NON-complement positions (appositives, adjuncts, free-standing elaborations). When the "that" clause is the direct object of a causative, aspectual, speech, or FEF verb, Rule 17 applies and the clause merges regardless of its cataphoric content.

**Rule 18: Fixed Idiom Integrity.**
Certain multi-word expressions are indivisible: "put to death," "from time to time," "prevailed upon," "put an end to," "one with another," **"it is expedient that"** (absorbed from former Rule 2, 2026-04-18). Never break inside a fixed idiom regardless of line length.
*Serves:* Criterion 1 (atomic thought).
*Note:* Corpus scan (Class D, 2026-04-13) confirmed zero violations — Rule 18 has been applied cleanly corpus-wide. "Expedient that" absorbed 2026-04-18 during canon audit — no independent rule logic remained after examination (every former-Rule-2 instance was a Rule 18 fixed-idiom instance).
*Example:* "I say unto you that it is expedient that ye should keep the law of Moses as yet" (2 Ne 25:16) — "it is expedient that" stays together per Rule 18.

**Rule 19: Cataphoric "That" Clauses Break; Anaphoric Merge.**
A "that" clause earns its own line only if it introduces at least one new referent, image, or proposition (cataphoric — forward-pointing, new content). If its content is entirely backward-pointing — pronouns, demonstratives, "the case," "this thing," "the same" — it stays merged (anaphoric — resolving, no new content).
*Serves:* Criterion 1 (atomic thought) and Criterion 2 (new image).
*Sharpened test:* A "that" clause is anaphoric (merge) ONLY when BOTH the subject AND the predicate are backward-pointing. If the subject points back ("this," "it") but the predicate introduces new content ("is by his grace," "is good"), the clause is cataphoric enough to break.
*Example (cataphoric, break):* "I say unto you / that the good shepherd doth call you" — new image, new action.
*Example (anaphoric, merge):* "The Spirit hath not said unto me that this should be the case" — "this" and "the case" both point back.
*Refinement:* Expletive "it" in cleft constructions ("that it is by his grace") is NOT anaphoric — it is a structural placeholder. Result/purpose clauses with new predication ("that it is good") are cataphoric. See Section 10 for the full refinement history.
*Precedence with Rule 17:* Rule 17 (Complement Integrity) trumps Rule 19 when both apply. If a cataphoric "that" clause is the direct object of a Rule 17 governing verb (causative, aspectual, speech in indirect discourse, or FEF), it merges regardless of its cataphoric status. Rule 19 governs cataphoric "that" clauses in NON-complement positions — appositives to nouns, adjuncts, free-standing elaborations. Example: "I say unto you that the time shall come" is cataphoric (new content) but merges because "say" is a speech verb taking "that" as complement (Rule 17). In contrast, "this is the sign, that a new star shall arise" is cataphoric and appositive to "the sign" (Rule 19 breaks).

**Rule 20: The No-Anchor Rule.**
Every sense-line must carry at least one thought-marking anchor: (1) a finite verb, (2) an infinitive, (3) a participle standing as predicate, or (4) a substantive head that is the independently predicated topic of its own line.
*Serves:* Criterion 1 (atomic thought) — this rule operationalizes the DEFAULT case of criterion 1 (the predication test).
*Critical clarification:* A "substantive head" does NOT include bare noun phrases that continue a prior clause's predicate as list objects or appositional extensions. A line like "and also her mistress, the queen, and the king," fails the anchor test even though it contains nouns — those nouns are objects of the previous line's verb.
*Exemptions:* (a) Single-line verses — atomic by definition. (b) Speech-intro prefixes. (c) Standalone sentence connectives ("Wherefore," "And now," "Therefore"). (d) Lines that fail the anchor test but pass the two-prong exception test (Section 2) — formally-marked parallel series members, portrait-building accumulations, speech-act announcements, and classical commata may legitimately lack a traditional anchor.
*Corpus status (2026-04-13):* 28,683 lines scanned, 5 unanchored (99.98% compliance).
*Source:* Imported from Reader's GNT project.

**Rule 21: Participial Absolute Integrity.**
A participial absolute — a subject-bearing participial clause of the form "X having Y-ed," or "X being Y," — constitutes a grammatically independent predication and earns its own sense-line. It is not a dangling modifier on the next clause's main verb but a full prosodic frame in its own right.
*Serves:* Criterion 1 (atomic thought).
*Diagnostic test:* Can the participial clause be rewritten as a finite sentence — "X was Y" or "X had Y-ed" — that stands alone as a complete thought? If yes, it is an absolute and earns its own line.
*Example (earns own line):* "I, Nephi, having been born of goodly parents," / "therefore I was taught..." (1 Ne 1:1) — "I, Nephi, had been born of goodly parents" stands alone. Line N+1 opens with "therefore" (rhetorical pivot + resumptive pronoun).
*Example (merge — genuinely subordinate):* "And yet, I being over-zealous to inherit the land of our fathers, collected as many as were desirous..." (Mos 9:3) — "collected" has no resumptive subject; the participial is the subject of the main verb.
*Relationship to the Goldilocks refinement:* Rule 21 is the principle; the Q1/Q2 diagnostic (see the Goldilocks Diagnostic in Section 4) is the operational test. Container-not-originator applies only to genuinely subordinating syntax, not to coordinated participial frames.
*Source:* Ported from GNT Reader (genitive absolute).

**Rule 22: Divine Title Appositives — Introducing vs. Referencing.**
Divine title appositive constructions ("Jesus Christ, the Son of God") are treated differently based on whether the speaker is INTRODUCING or REFERENCING the identity.
*Serves:* Criterion 1 (atomic thought) and Criterion 2 (image).

**Diagnostic sharpened 2026-04-18 (over-structuring audit pruning intuition-basis):** INTRODUCING earns a stacked split ONLY when one of three formal anchors is present. Absent a formal anchor, the default is MERGE.

**INTRODUCING (stack) — requires formal anchor:**
1. **Formal naming formula:** "his name shall be called [X], [title]" / "and they shall call his name [X], [title]" — the formula itself signals prophetic naming. *Example:* "his name shall be Jesus Christ, / the Son of God" (2 Ne 25:19).
2. **First-occurrence context:** the identity is being revealed for the first time in the passage. Can be tested by checking whether the preceding verses have already established the identity.
3. **Prophetic proclamation frame:** the speech is a prophetic declaration marked by formal frame ("Thus saith the Lord," "Behold, I say unto you," recorded vision, angel's announcement). The appositive functions as part of the prophetic revelation.

**REFERENCING (merge) — default:** The speaker is using an already-established identity as a name unit.
*Example:* "I am a disciple of Jesus Christ, the Son of God" (3 Ne 5:13).

**If no formal anchor is present, default to MERGE.** This sharpening prevents the rule from sliding into "it felt like a reveal" intuition — the over-structuring audit flagged the old diagnostic as judgment-heavy. The three formal anchors above are mechanically identifiable and should carry the evidentiary burden for a split.

**Boundary case:** repeated invocations of a title within a single passage (e.g., a sermon) should settle on ONE treatment throughout — either all stacked (if the passage is prophetic-declarative) or all merged (if the passage is referential). Do not oscillate within a unified rhetorical beat.

*Corpus:* 38 divine title appositives. Pre-sharpening: 18+6 stacked (INTRODUCING), 14 merged (REFERENCING). Post-sharpening: re-audit pending to verify each stacked case has a formal anchor; any stacked case without a formal anchor is a merge candidate.

**Rule 23: Narrowing Vocative Addresses.**
Compound vocative addresses that narrow from broad group to specific subset earn a break at the narrowing point.
*Serves:* Criterion 2 (image — zoom effect).
*Break (narrows):* "O ye house of Israel, / all ye that are broken off and are driven out" — nation to diaspora subset.
*Keep merged (restates at same level):* "O ye my people, or my brethren," — equivalence restatement (Rule 5), same scope.
*Corpus observation:* 15 instances. The BofM text consistently narrows from broad to specific — never the reverse.

**Rule 24 — SUPERSEDED 2026-04-18 by the unified "Which"-Clause Decision Tree** (see Section 8). The equation/description distinction Rule 24 encoded is absorbed into the unified tree's pattern taxonomy:

- **Predicative identifier ("which is X" where X classifies/names)** → MERGE. This covers most of Rule 24's "description" cases AND the "equation" cases that today's audit ruled should merge (e.g., "commandment which is the word of God," "lake of fire and brimstone which is the second death").
- **Non-restrictive relative introducing new information, new action, or consequence** → SPLIT. This covers Rule 24's genuine "new info" cases ("the atonement, / which was prepared from the foundation of the world").

Rule 24's original equation/description binary gave *equation → BREAK* as default, but today's corpus review (commit `8174838`, 9 merges) applied the three unified rules (Class P + identifier/new-info + Syntactic Affirmation) and converged on MERGE for predicative identifiers regardless of their equation-vs-description classification. The finer distinction Rule 24 made turned out not to be load-bearing once the broader syntactic-affirmation framework was in place.

*Historical corpus observation preserved:* 65 instances of "which is/are" predicatives; pre-2026-04-18 treatment had 52+5 broken (equation) and 8 merged (descriptive). Post-2026-04-18 treatment: default MERGE per the predicative-identifier row of the unified decision tree; SPLIT only when the "which" clause introduces a new action or substantive predication (not mere identification).

*Worked examples:*
- *"lake of fire and brimstone which is the second death"* — merged (predicative identifier, unified tree)
- *"the atonement, / which was prepared from the foundation of the world"* — split (non-restrictive new info, unified tree)

**Rule 26: Adjective + "That" Complement Stays Together.**
When an adjective requires a "that" clause as its complement, they stay on the same line. The adjective is incomplete without it.
*Serves:* Criterion 1 (atomic thought) and Criterion 4 (syntax).
*Merge:* "if it were possible that our first parents..." — possible WHAT? "It is expedient that much should be done..." — expedient THAT what?
*Break (verb is complete without "that"):* "he hath said / that no unclean thing..." — "said" is complete as a speech act.
*The distinction:* Adjectives (possible, expedient, desirous, necessary, needful, impossible) REQUIRE their "that" clause. Verbs of speaking/perceiving may be complete without specifying content.

### Editorial Principles (defensible, documented, require judgment)

**Rule 4: Circumstantial Clause Pairing.**
Paired circumstantial phrases describing the same condition should stay on one line.
*Serves:* Criterion 1 (atomic thought).
*Example:* "in their ignorance, not having salvation declared unto them" (Mosiah 15:24) — two descriptions of the same circumstance.

**Rule 8: Framing Devices Attach to Content.**
Discourse-opening markers attach to the first line of their content — do not orphan them as standalone lines.

**Two sub-classes (refined 2026-04-18 FUZZY revisit):**
1. **Intro-only framing markers:** "For behold," "Behold," "But behold," "And thus," speech introduction formulas. These NEVER stand alone — they are mid-thought and must lead their content.
2. **Standalone sentence connectives:** "And now," "Wherefore," "Therefore," "Nevertheless" may stand alone per Rule 20 Exemption (c). When they do, the following line is their content and must be a complete atomic thought without the connective.

*Serves:* Criterion 1 (atomic thought).

*Breath exception DROPPED 2026-04-18.* The prior exception ("when combined line exceeds a natural breath, the frame may stand alone") imported the non-mechanical Breath Test into an otherwise mechanical rule. The replacement diagnostic is Criterion 1: if frame + content form one integrated atomic thought, they stay together regardless of length; if they are genuinely two thoughts, split at the thought-boundary, not at an arbitrary breath-limit.

**Rule 10: Never Split Verb from Direct Object.**
A governing verb and its bare direct-object noun phrase stay on the same line. When a prep-phrase or adverbial intervenes ("retained in remembrance the captivity of your fathers"), the verb + PP + DO are one unit. Do not break at "in remembrance / the captivity."
*Serves:* Criterion 1 (atomic thought) and Criterion 4 (syntax).

**Reinstated 2026-04-18 after cull was found premature.** The earlier cull ("Rule 17 + Rule 20 cover this") was wrong:
- Rule 17 only catches "that"-complements, infinitives, and FEF extrapositions — NOT bare-NP direct objects.
- Rule 20's anchor scanner has a blind spot: it treats any noun on a bare-DO continuation line as a "substantive head" and gives it a pass, even though the 2026-04-15 tightening note explicitly excluded "bare noun phrases that continue a prior clause's predicate as list objects or appositional extensions." The scanner logic doesn't enforce the tightening.
- The territory Rule 10 catches — verb + direct-object splits across a line boundary with or without intervening adverbial — is genuinely uncovered by the validator suite.

**Diagnostic:** Is the line below a bare NP (determiner + noun + optional PP/relative) that is the syntactic direct object of a verb on the prior line? If yes, merge. The intervening adverbial or PP belongs with its verb.

*Canonical trigger (2026-04-18 Alma 5:6):* "have you sufficiently retained in remembrance / the captivity of your fathers?" — the DO "the captivity of your fathers" is bound to "retained"; the intervening "in remembrance" doesn't license a break before the DO.

*Scope clarification — NOT covered by Rule 10:* Already-complete clauses followed by a restrictive or non-restrictive relative (handled by unified "Which"-clause decision tree or Class P); subject-NP continuations with their own predication to follow (Rule 20 handles); parallel coordinate object series (unless case #1 formally-marked parallel series).

**Validator:** `validators/validate_rule_10_verb_do_split.py` (2026-04-18).

**Rule 13 — REDUCED TO POINTER 2026-04-18.** Parallel structures stack vertically when each element passes the two-prong exception test — see **Section 2, "Unless" Justification #1 (Formally-Marked Parallel Series)**. Stacking that produces fragment lines fails the two-prong test (fragments fail the cognitive prong), so the "if each element is independently atomic" constraint is already encoded in the default+unless framework. No independent rule logic remains; flagged by redundancy audit as co-extensive with Justification #1.

*Worked example preserved — 2 Ne 19:2 (Isaiah 9:2):* The four-line version splitting each Hebrew colon at the subject-verb seam produces hanging subjects. Corrected to two lines, each independently atomic: "The people that walked in darkness have seen a great light; / they that dwell in the land of the shadow of death, upon them hath the light shined." This example demonstrates Justification #1's cognitive prong in action.

**Rule 14 — CULLED 2026-04-18.** "Escalatory vs. restrictive" is a semantic/rhetorical register judgment that reduces to Criterion 1 (atomic thought) + Criterion 2 (single image) applied directly. The classification requires asking "is this one thought or two?" (Criterion 1) and "does this push to a new scene?" (Criterion 2) — both already in the core hierarchy. A rule that merely relabels the hierarchy test on a specific surface pattern doesn't earn its place. Flagged by over-structuring audit as judgment-dressed-as-rule. The Mosiah 3:7 "except it be unto death" worked example is preserved under Criterion 2 as the escalation-earns-split case.

**"According to" — Manner vs. Source/Authority.**
"According to" phrases split when they claim divine source or authority; they merge when they describe manner.
*Serves:* Criterion 1 (atomic thought).
*Merge (manner — HOW):* "spoke unto them, according to his word."
*Split (source/authority — BY WHAT POWER):* "it whispereth me, / according to the workings of the Spirit of the Lord."
*Test:* Is the "according to" phrase answering HOW? (manner, merge) or BY WHAT AUTHORITY / FROM WHAT SOURCE? (authorization, split).

**EP-2 ("Through" as Source/Mechanism) — CULLED 2026-04-18.** Was one worked example of EP-1 applied to a different preposition. The manner/authority distinction is the same; only the preposition differs. Absorbed into EP-1 ("According to") as an example: *"redeemed of God, / through the blood of the Lamb"* — the "through" phrase claims the mechanism of salvation (source/authority), parallel to EP-1's "according to" source/authority split. Flagged by over-structuring audit as one-preposition-worked-example pretending to be a distinct rule.

**Inverted Predicate Constructions.**
When the predicate is fronted for emphasis — "great is my joy," "blessed are they" — break before the inverted predicate. The inversion signals rhetorical weight.
*Serves:* Criterion 2 (image) and Criterion 3 (breath).
*Test:* Can you rephrase in normal word order ("my joy is great") and the emphasis is lost? If yes, the inversion earns its own line.

**Title/Role Stays with Its Domain.**
Never split a title or role designation from the prepositional phrase that completes it: "high priest over the church," "king over the land."
*Serves:* Criterion 1 (atomic thought) and Criterion 4 (syntax).
*Test:* Would the title alone be ambiguous or incomplete?

**Virtue/Vice Lists — Examine for Parallel Structure.**
When a passage stacks moral qualities, examine the list for detectable parallel patterns. Common patterns: dual parallels (2-beat pairs), triadic parallels (3-beat triads), dual + expansion (2+1 pattern), crescendo lists (items grow progressively longer).
*Serves:* Criterion 2 (image) and Criterion 3 (breath).
*Test:* Is there a detectable rhythmic pattern? If yes, line breaks should make it visible. If no pattern is detectable, default to the breath/image test. Do not impose parallelism that is not there.

### Guidelines (useful tendencies)

**Line Length as a Signal, Not a Rule.**
Short, staccato lines create emphasis and slow the reader down. Long lines create flow and momentum. Neither is wrong. There is no hard character count threshold. Stan: "line/character limit should not be arbitrary — if something's not an atomic thought, it's not."

**Vocative Splitting (Nuanced).**
Vocative-as-command gets its own line ("Awake, my soul!"). Vocative-as-appeal stays with the request ("O Lord, wilt thou redeem my soul?" is one atomic cry). "Wo unto X who Y" — merge when Y defines X; split when Y elaborates.

**Compound List Break Signals.**
In a compound list governed by one preposition or verb, bare "and [noun]" items are compound objects and stay merged. A break is justified only with a grammatical signal:
1. Elided auxiliary + stacked participles (each is an implied predication)
2. Possessive restart ("and his" appearing after items without possessive, or changing possessor)
3. Demonstrative ("and that/this/these" signals a new specified noun phrase)
4. Relative clause attached ("which is/who was" adds a predication)

Without such a signal, bare "and [noun]" items merge. A repeated identical possessive ("and his X, and his Y, and his Z") is formulaic and does NOT alone justify stacking. A possessive RESTART (appearing after items without it, or changing from one possessor to another) justifies a break.

**Fronted Adverbials.**
Short lines that appear to be orphan fragments may instead be fronted elements occupying a genuine clause-initial position. "With power" in Mosiah 3:5 is a fronted adverbial — the fronting is the device. Check deep structure before proposing a merge.

**Line Reordering.**
Reordering lines within a verse is a legitimate but sparing editorial move when the logical or rhetorical arc requires it (established from Mosiah 15:9).

### Eliminated Rules

**Rule 25: "And Thus We See" — ELIMINATED 2026-03-29.**
The editorial narrator formula "And thus we see / that [lesson]" was re-merged across the corpus. "We see" without its complement clause is an incomplete thought — it fails the atomic thought test. 13 instances merged. The distribution of "and thus we see" remains valid stylometric data.

**Methodological principle established from this elimination:** When discourse-marker status (prosody) and syntactic completeness (atomic thought) conflict, syntactic completeness governs unless the prosodic break is doing clear, significant, unambiguous rhetorical or structural work.

## Section 4: Operational Tests

These are the instruments trench Claudes actually run during editorial work.

### The Rule 20 Anchor Test

**What it checks:** Does every sense-line carry at least one thought-marking anchor (finite verb, infinitive, predicate participle, or independently-predicated substantive head)?

**How to run it:** For each line, look for one of the four anchor types. If none is present, the line is a candidate for merging with its neighbor.

**Updated exemption list:**
1. Single-line verses (atomic by definition)
2. Speech-intro prefixes
3. Standalone sentence connectives ("Wherefore," "And now," "Therefore")
4. Lines that fail the anchor test but pass the two-prong exception test (Section 2):
   - Formally-marked parallel series members (e.g., "and scourged," in a polysyndetic passion list)
   - Portrait-building attribute accumulations
   - Speech-act announcements
   - Classical commata ("Yea." / "If not so,")

**Important:** Rule 20 operationalizes the DEFAULT case of criterion 1. It is a mechanical proxy — necessary but not sufficient for a merge decision. A line that lacks a thought-marking anchor almost always fails criterion 1, but a line that passes criterion 1 via the two-prong exception test may legitimately lack a traditional anchor.

**Object-continuation failure mode:** The most common way a noun-only line slips through the anchor check is as a compound-list continuation: the previous line establishes a verb, and the following line names additional objects. These look anchored (they have nouns) but are not.

### The Period Test

**Revised 2026-04-18 after Rule 17 generalization.** The Period Test was originally a syntactic-optionality test distinguishing "obligatory" from "optional" complements. That framing was superseded when Rule 17 (Complement Integrity) generalized to cover speech, cognition, and volition verbs — all of which take "optional" complements syntactically but whose predication is semantically incomplete without the "that" clause when supplied.

**What it now checks:** When a verb takes a "that" complement, does the verb+complement form one integrated proposition? If yes, Rule 17 merges them regardless of whether the complement is strictly syntactically required.

**How to run it:** Identify the verb's class (see Section 8 "'That'-Clause Taxonomy"). If it falls under Rule 17's classes (causative, aspectual, speech-in-indirect-discourse, cognition, volition, FEF extraposition), the complement merges. Apply Rule 17's exception filters (direct discourse, AICTP, purpose clause, meta-announcement, parallel series, recitativum).

**Updated verb-class table (per Rule 17 generalization):**

| Verb class | Examples | Treatment | Rule basis |
|-----------|----------|-----------|-----------|
| Speech (indirect discourse) | say, testify, declare, prophesy, spake, swear, proclaim, tell, confess, rehearse | MERGE | Rule 17 (extended 2026-04-17) |
| Cognition | know, believe, perceive, remember, see/hear (cognitive) | MERGE | Rule 17 (extended 2026-04-17) |
| Volition | wish, desire, hope, pray (petition), trust | MERGE | Rule 17 (extended 2026-04-17) |
| Cognitive (obligatory-syntactic) | suppose, understand, imagine | MERGE | Rule 17 |
| Causative | cause, command, grant, suffer, permit | MERGE | Rule 17 |
| Demonstrative | show (prove sense), witness, manifest | MERGE | Rule 17 |
| Purpose-introducing "that" | "that they might," "that ye may" | BREAK | Rule 7 (purpose clauses) |
| AICTP "that" | "it came to pass that" | BREAK | Rule 16 (AICTP-dangling-that) |

**Direct discourse with colon/voice shift, meta-announcement, parallel "that" series, and divine recitativum are Rule 17 exception classes — see Rule 17's full exception list.**

**Stale rows removed:** prior table classified "reporting/speech" and "perception" verbs as "Optional / YES break." Those rows were superseded by the 2026-04-17 Rule 17 generalization and are kept here only as a historical note. The superseded classification produced the 50/50 split on "I say unto you" constructions that the 2026-04-17 sweep resolved via commit `b04cae9` (38 speech-verb merges).

### The Image Test

**What it checks:** Does a line contain one image or two? Does an "insomuch that" clause introduce a new scene?

**How to run it:** Close your eyes and picture the scene. Does the line make you see ONE thing? If two distinct images, candidate for splitting. If no complete image, candidate for merging.

**For "insomuch that" specifically:** Does the clause make you SEE something new (a new body position, a new physical event, a new scene) or does it just give you MORE DETAIL about what you were already picturing? New image = break. Same image with more detail = merge.

### The Breath Test

**What it checks:** Can the line be delivered in one breath at natural reading pace?

**How to run it:** Read it aloud (or imagine reading aloud). If you gasp, it is too long. If you have nowhere to land, it is too short.

**Honest scope:** Currently no falsifiable threshold. Functions as a sanity check, not a mechanical gate. When a merge produces something unspeakable, coordinating syntax was almost certainly flattened.

**Important:** Atomic thought (Criterion 1) trumps length. A line that passes Criterion 1 as one integrated thought may be legitimately long if the thought itself is long. Example: Alma 10:27 at 29 words merges as one indirect-discourse complement (Rule 17) because the thought is atomic, even though the line is on the long side. The breath test's role is to flag when length SIGNALS a likely thought-compression problem — not to cap atomic-thought lines by word count.

### The Two-Prong Exception Test

**What it checks:** Should a line that fails the predication test still stand?

**How to run it:** Cross-reference to Section 2. Both prongs must pass:
1. Cognitive: Is this a demonstrable idea unit?
2. Structural: Is there a formal justification (one of the four structural justifications)?

If either fails, merge.

### The Subordinating vs. Coordinating Diagnostic (Q1/Q2)

**What it checks:** When evaluating a candidate merge, is the syntax subordinating (merge) or coordinating (split)?

**Q1.** Can line N be read as a standalone prosodic predication (with an implicit "was/had" if needed)?
- "he being an exceedingly curious man" -> "he was an exceedingly curious man" = YES, standalone
- "the Lamanites and the Amalekites and the people of Amulon" -> cannot stand alone, needs a verb = NO

**Q2.** Does line N+1 open with a rhetorical pivot, a resumptive subject pronoun, or a parallel conjunction?
- "therefore he went forth" = YES (pivot + resumptive)
- "had built a great city" = NO (bare finite verb directly picking up the subject)

**If Q1=yes AND Q2=yes -> COORDINATING, do NOT merge.**
**If Q1=no OR Q2=no -> SUBORDINATING, merge is correct.**

**Signatures of coordinate syntax (red flags for merge):**
- "therefore" / "thus" / "wherefore" / "and now" opening line N+1
- Resumptive subject pronoun at start of line N+1
- New grammatical subject NP opening line N+1
- Parallel conjunction "or" + determiner
- "having/being" + full predicate on line N (stands alone as "was/had")
- Subject-stacks of 3+ parallel NPs (theological triads, ethnic lists, casualty rolls)

**Signatures of subordinating syntax (green lights for merge):**
- Line N ends in a bare preposition waiting for its object
- Line N ends in a verb missing its direct object
- Line N ends in an NP subject with no finite verb
- Line N ends in a speech/cognition verb awaiting content
- Line N ends in a "hollow head" ("began to fear exceedingly / lest...")
- Line N+1 opens with a bare finite verb that directly picks up line N's subject with no pivot

### The Completing Test (for Class P — Relative Clauses)

**What it checks:** Is a relative clause completing an identification (merge) or extending with new information (split)?

**Three diagnostic tests:**
1. **Completing test (primary).** Remove the relative clause. Does the head noun stand alone as an atomic thought? If NO, the relative is completing. Merge.
2. **Paraphrase test.** Can the relative clause be replaced by an adjective? ("a man whose name was Sherem" -> "a named man") If YES, it is restrictive/identifying. Merge.
3. **New image test.** Does the relative clause paint a new scene beyond identification? If YES, it may be cataphoric and can remain split.

**It-cleft constructions always merge.** "It was X who Y" / "they are they who Y" — the relative clause IS the obligatory predication.

## Section 5: FEF Framework

### What Is a Front-End Frame?

A **Front-End Frame (FEF)** is a structure we discovered through consistent editorial application of the atomic-thought test. FEF is our coined term — it describes a pattern that emerged from the English text, not one we imported from a theoretical framework. A line is an FEF when it meets all four criteria:

1. **Position:** Opens a verse or verse-block (first line after a verse marker).
2. **Discourse marker:** Begins with one of: AICTP-like structures ("And it came to pass"), "And now," "Wherefore," "Therefore," "For behold," "Behold," "Nevertheless," "And thus," "But behold," "Now," or a speech introduction formula.
3. **Irreducibility:** Binds syntactically to at least one dependent element (temporal clause, spatial clause, participial phrase, relative clause, or object clause) such that no break point exists that would not orphan either the frame or its content under the atomic-image rule.
4. **Expansion:** Is followed by two or more shorter lines that unpack, elaborate, or detail the frame's content.

The irreducibility criterion (3) is what distinguishes FEFs from merely long lines. A line can be long because it contains a list or compound predicate — that is breakable. An FEF is long because every element is syntactically dependent on every other element. The frame suspends resolution until the main verb arrives, and everything between the discourse marker and the main verb is part of the suspension.

### FEFs and Their Structural Analogue (Interpretive Observation — Not Part of the Method)

The following is an interpretive observation about the FEF pattern, not a methodological rule. The FEF definition and editorial treatment above stand independently of whether this observation is accepted.

We recognized FEFs as a structure in the BofM text through consistent editorial application of the atomic-thought test. We subsequently noted that this structure closely corresponds to a biblical structure found in both the KJV and the Tanakh. The wayəhî + temporal clause pattern in Hebrew narrative establishes time, place, condition, and actor in a front-loaded frame before the main verb resolves:

```
wayəhî [temporal clause] [circumstantial clause]... [main verb]
```

(Our AICTP-like structures are functionally analogous to this Hebrew pattern.) The FEF pattern was not discovered by starting from Hebrew grammar and looking for it in English. It was discovered by applying an English readability criterion (atomic image / atomic breath) consistently across the text, noticing that certain lines resisted breaking, and then recognizing that the resistant structures correspond to Hebrew narrative conventions.

**Theory-neutral caveat:** Whether the Book of Mormon's source is ancient Hebrew-influenced text or 19th-century KJV-absorbed composition, the structural pattern is real and measurable. The FEF definition is theory-neutral — it describes what is on the page without presupposing an explanation. FEFs provide several functions, including those analogous to a Hebraic temporal frame construction (the wayəhî + temporal clause pattern that sets the scene before the main verb resolves).

**Important distinction:** The FEF *method* (how we identify and format these structures) is Part 1 material — it applies to any English text with front-loaded framing constructions. The Hebrew *correspondence* (the observation that FEFs match wayəhî patterns) is Part 2 material — it is an interpretive observation about this particular corpus. A skeptical reader can accept the method while rejecting the correspondence, and the editorial output remains identical.

### FEF vs. Speech Attribution Asymmetry

**FEFs are the narrator's voice.** "For behold," "And now," "Wherefore," "Nevertheless" — these are the literary narrator (Mormon, Nephi, Alma) shaping discourse. They attach to what they introduce because narrator and content are continuous.

**Speech attribution formulas introduce a different voice.** "And again:" "Thus saith the Lord:" "He said:" — these are handoffs to quoted speech. The colon is the textual signal. Rule: attribution formula takes its own line; quoted content begins fresh on the next line. This is not FEF behavior — it is the opposite. FEFs bind forward; speech attributions release into a new voice.

The literary narrator vs. oracular voice asymmetry: the narrator deploys FEFs (orienting, reasoning, persuading, connecting — markers of a literary mind at work). Divine speech quotes short and compressed — it declares and stops. FEFs cluster in narrative voice; direct divine speech tends toward shorter, less-structured lines.

### FEF Temporal Frame Principle

**The refined rule: complete the temporal/genitive reference on one line, then break before the main verb.**

The FEF frame ends where the temporal identification ends. "And it came to pass in the seventh year of the reign of the judges" is the frame. "There were about three thousand five hundred souls..." is the expansion. The frame can be long — that is fine. But the main verb is where expansion begins, and expansion earns its own line.

This principle is structural, not authorial — it operates the same way whether Nephi or Mormon is deploying it.

### FEF-Aware Pre-Breaking

The current v8 reformatter breaks at syntactic boundaries without recognizing framing constructions, so it frequently breaks FEFs that then require manual re-merging. An FEF-aware pre-breaker would:

1. Detect the discourse marker at the start of a verse
2. Walk forward looking for the main verb
3. Protect everything between marker and main verb resolution as one atomic unit
4. Apply normal breaking rules only to the expansion lines after the FEF resolves

### FEF Connection to OTC

OTC (Oral Transmission Criticism, Stan's doctoral dissertation) argues that colometry tells us *where* oral units are; OTC tells us *why* they are there. The BOM Reader's colometric work is an unintentional pilot study demonstrating that consistent editorial colometric criteria applied at scale produce author-differentiated results. FEFs are the colometric analogue of OTC's "oral ridges" (zones of high mnemonic density that resist editorial simplification).

## Section 6: Literature and Terminology

### Marschall Comparison

**Priscille Marschall (2023)** — "Refining the Criteria for Delineating Cola and Periods."

**What we share:** Her key concept "semantico-syntactic completeness" is equivalent to our "atomic thought" test. She validates that phrases without verbs CAN be cola if they carry enough semantic weight — this legitimizes our short emphatic lines as classical "commata" (our structural justification 4). She distinguishes "autonomous" cola (disjointed style, each stands alone) from "periodic" cola (linked within a period by parallelism, hyperbaton, etc.).

**Where we diverge:** She is grounded in Graeco-Roman rhetorical treatises (Aristotle, Pseudo-Demetrius, Cicero, Quintilian). We are grounded in cognitive processing (Chafe's idea units, working-memory chunking, Kintsch's propositions). The groundings answer different questions: hers asks what ancient rhetoricians considered a well-formed colon; ours asks what a modern reader can process as one cognitive bite.

**The relationship is complementary, not competitive.** Marschall provides classical validation — her work confirms that our editorial output produces units consistent with ancient colometric theory. We provide operational precision — four ranked criteria, mechanical validation, empirical voice-differentiation confirmation. She describes; we operationalize. A paper should cite both traditions, not position one as superior. Her "almost any constituent can, under the right circumstances, be a colon" is the same insight as our two-prong exception test — we specify what those circumstances are; she shows the classical tradition already recognized them.

**For our specific purpose** (building a reading edition optimized for ESL readers, children, and newcomers), the cognitive grounding is more directly applicable because we are optimizing for the reader's processing, not for conformity with classical rhetorical categories. But this is a difference of purpose, not of correctness.

### The "Imposing vs. Revealing" Principle

Line breaks follow grammatical structure that already exists in the text. Breaking at causal clauses, purpose clauses, relative clauses, and parallel structures makes visible what is already encoded. The breaks are descriptive, not interpretive.

When identifying the grammatical basis for a line break: use standard grammatical terms when confident, say "I think this might be [X] but I'm not certain" when pattern-matching, and say "I don't know" when uncertain. Do not construct grammatical categories to justify editorial instincts. If a break cannot be named in standard grammatical terms, it is an editorial decision — which is fine, but should be labeled honestly.

### The Syntactic Affirmation Test for Line Boundaries

**The sharpest operationalization of "reveal not impose":** every line break must be affirmed by a syntactic feature of English. A boundary is licensed when the syntax itself creates a separation — appositive structure, subordinate clause introduction, formally-marked coordination, complement-clause independence, participial absolute, relative clause on an already-complete predication, or similar recognized grammatical category. If no syntactic feature affirms the boundary, the break is imposed, not revealed.

**Syntax is the floor.** The colometric criteria (atomic thought, single image, breath) operate **within** what syntax licenses, never against it. Criteria 1–3 choose between options that syntax has already made available; they never create boundaries the grammar doesn't support.

**The test** (when considering a split): can you name the syntactic feature that creates this boundary? If yes, state it (e.g., "appositive 'that' clause on a cataphoric noun," "formally-marked parallel coordination with repeated conjunction," "subordinate purpose clause introduced by 'that ye may'"). If you cannot name a grammatical feature, the break is imposed.

**Retroactively catches these failure modes** that earlier sessions encountered:
- *"Long interjection earns split"* — a manner qualifier is syntactically bound to its verb; no boundary. Imposing.
- *"Rhetorical force earns split"* — rhetorical impact is not a syntactic feature. Imposing.
- *"Em-dash as diagnostic evidence"* — punctuation is not syntax (see also the Punctuation Must Not Have Deterministic Force principle immediately below). Imposing.
- *"Recognized theological formula earns its own line"* — theological category is external framework, not English syntax. Imposing.

All four are the same failure mode at different surface forms: reaching for aesthetic, rhetorical, punctuational, or doctrinal justification before checking whether syntax actually affirms the boundary.

**Anchor case:** Alma 22:13 (2026-04-18) — "and also the plan of redemption, which was prepared from the foundation of the world, through Christ, for all whosoever would believe on his name." The beneficiary PP "for all whosoever would believe on his name" is syntactically bound to the passive verb "prepared" (prepared *for* [beneficiary]); there is no syntactic feature creating a boundary between "through Christ," and "for all whosoever." Despite being a recognized BofM soteriological formula, it merges with the governing clause because syntax does not affirm a break. The theological weight of a phrase is not a valid basis for a line break.

### Punctuation Must Not Have Deterministic Force

The commas, semicolons, colons, and dashes in the canonical text are editorial additions — added by the printer/publisher, not original to the translation event. They may corroborate a line-break decision but CANNOT justify one by themselves.

**The test:** If a line break depends on a comma being present to make the case, remove the comma mentally and ask whether the break still holds on purely grammatical/rhetorical grounds.

### The Cognitive-Grounding Test

A filter on future rule adoption: when a proposed rule or principle is advanced, ask whether it is grounded in cognitive processing (how readers chunk and comprehend text) or in external theoretical frameworks (rhetorical treatises, compositional training hypotheses). If the latter, it needs stronger justification to be adopted. This filter guards against the tendency to over-adopt external rhetorical-treatise frameworks.

### Rhetorical Force Is Not Atomic-Thought Independence

**Principle (Stan's explicit correction, 2026-04-16):** "Rhetorical force" — the sense that a line feels powerful, emphatic, or aesthetically complete on its own — is not by itself a justification for keeping a line split. The test for a valid split is always Criterion 1: does the line carry its own atomic thought? If the line is grammatically the indirect object, the direct object, or a coordinate member under a governing verb with no independent predication, its rhetorical impact when severed doesn't make it an atomic thought.

**Stan verbatim:** *"I am not interested in rhetorical force per se, unless we are saying that it creates a separate thought-line unit."*

**Worked example (FAIL — rhetorical force alone):** In Alma 17:14 "for they had undertaken to preach the word of God / to a wild and a hardened and a ferocious people" — the polysyndetic triple "a wild and a hardened and a ferocious" has strong rhetorical force, but it is grammatically the indirect object of "preach unto," not an independent thought. The aesthetic power of stacking doesn't override its grammatical dependence on the main clause. MERGE.

**Worked example (PASS — genuine thought-independence):** In Alma 20:30 "and had driven them from house to house, / and from place to place" — the parallel PP is grammatically bound to "had driven" but creates a genuine second image (intimate door-to-door persecution → broader geographic displacement). Stan called this "the premier readjusted camera angle example." KEEP SPLIT — not because the split sounds good, but because the reader's mind's eye genuinely repositions between the two frames. Criterion 2 (single image) earns the split, not rhetorical force.

**Implication for editorial discipline:** When tempted to keep a split for aesthetic reasons, name the criterion that earns it. If the only answer is "it sounds powerful," the split is likely over-imposed. If the answer is "it's a separate atomic thought" or "it's a separate image (camera repositioning)" or "it's one of the four structural justifications (unless cases)" — the split is principled.

### The Camera-Angle Diagnostic (Criterion 2 operationalization)

**When Criterion 2 (single image) earns a split:** Close your eyes and picture the scene described by each line. Does the reader's mind's eye move between distinct frames — different actors, different locations, different scales, different moments — or does it hold one continuous frame?

**If the mind's eye repositions ("camera moves"), Criterion 2 supports the split.** Each line paints a distinct picture.

**If the mind's eye holds one continuous frame, Criterion 2 does NOT earn the split.** Keep the lines merged; rhetorical amplification within one frame is not two images.

**Anchor case (camera repositions):** *"and had driven them from house to house, / and from place to place"* — line 1: close-up, door-to-door. Line 2: aerial view, geographic scale. Same verb, two camera positions. SPLIT legitimate.

**Counter-case (camera does not reposition):** *"a wild and a hardened and a ferocious people"* — three adjectives modifying one noun, painting one composite portrait of the same people. Same frame, same image, rhetorical intensification only. MERGE (not split).

**Practical test:** Can you describe in one sentence what the reader "sees" in each line? If yes and the descriptions are distinct scenes, camera moves — split supported. If no, camera stays — merge.

## Section 7: Cognitive Grounding

### How This Methodology Was Actually Developed

The methodology was not derived from psycholinguistic theory. It was developed empirically:

1. **Skousen-inspired.** Royal Skousen's sense-line formatting of the Book of Mormon demonstrated that colometric presentation was possible for this corpus, though his method relied on unstated editorial judgment and was not formally replicable.

2. **Developed through iterative editorial experimentation.** Stan developed the methodology through hands-on work — determining preliminary criteria for how to identify sense-line boundaries, testing line breaks, reading aloud, and then examining and refining what "one unit of meaning" actually means in practice. The four criteria, the rules, and the hierarchy emerged from this iterative process, not from a theoretical framework.

3. **Scholarly literature was not consulted until after experimentation.** I sought to develop a pragmatically coherent text that passed the "sanity check" phase before allowing any particular theory to cloud my editorial judgment. My assumption was that if I created a text that met a set of narrow, simple, and relatively self-obvious definitions of an "atomic thought unit," it would cohere with prevailing scholarly consensus. And indeed, points of convergence did seem to naturally emerge, where Chafe, Miller/Cowan, and Kintsch were identified as psycholinguistic literature that may explain *why* the empirically-developed methodology works. This is an opportunity for further theoretical grounding, not a claim of derivation. The methodology intriguingly converged with Chafe's idea-unit framework, just as the FEF pattern independently converged with Hebrew wayəhî conventions.

This genealogy matters for three reasons:

(a) It asserts that an effortless, thought-line-driven reading experience is the true measure of success in recovering the correct line breaks in the text.

(b) It assumes that cognitive, psycholinguistic, and biblical scholarship will align with this methodology if it is, in fact, accurately revealing the thought-line structure of the text rather than imposing it. In other words, this is not a test of anyone else's theory.

(c) Because of a and b, the convergences provide opportunity space for further research and experimentation.

### The Cognitive Cycle

The thinks → writes/dictates → hears/reads → thinks cycle is a pedagogical summary (not a formal processing model) that captures the emerging theoretical frame:

- The author produces language in idea-unit-sized chunks (Chafe)
- These chunks are encoded into syntactic structure (encoding)
- The reader decodes the syntax back into processing-unit-sized chunks (Kintsch, Daneman & Carpenter)

Colometric formatting is the editorial act of making the encoding visible — aligning the visual presentation with the production-level chunking so the reader's decoding is maximally efficient.

### The Dictation Hypothesis and Why Breath Sits Above Syntax

The Book of Mormon presents itself as a dictated text. If dictation is the composition mode, the breath unit is causally prior — the dictator speaks in breath-length chunks, and the scribe records them. Syntax is the structure within each breath, not the primary organizing principle. This is why breath sits above syntax in our hierarchy (criterion 3 above criterion 4), even though syntax is more mechanically testable.

Caveat: dictation studies (including Chafe's own work on differences between spoken and written language) show that dictated text is cognitively closer to written than to spontaneous speech — dictators plan ahead, revise, and can produce units longer than breath groups. The "breath-length chunks" assumption is a simplification. The stronger claim is: dictation preserves *more* production-level structure than polished written composition does, making breath a more relevant criterion for a dictated corpus than for a literary one.

### Psycholinguistic Literature (Opportunity for Further Grounding)

The following scholars provide literature that may explain why the empirically-developed methodology works. These are not the sources of the methodology — they are opportunities for further theoretical grounding identified after the editorial work was underway.

**Chafe's idea units** (1994): Each clause or phrase that a speaker produces in a single intonation contour corresponds to one active focus of consciousness. Our "atomic thought" test is an adaptation of this concept for written text. Important caveat: Chafe defines idea units by *prosodic* criteria (pause, intonation contour) in *spoken* discourse. We are applying the concept to *written* text where prosodic boundaries are not directly observable. The bridge is principled — written clause structure reflects underlying production-level chunking — but it is an extension, not a direct implementation. (Chafe 1980, "The Pear Stories," provides the most direct connection between idea units and clause structure. Chafe 1994 ch. 3 is the defining treatment and should be read before any prospectus-level claims about this grounding.)

**Chunking and working memory:** The concept of "chunking" — that working memory organizes information into discrete units — derives from Miller (1956) on memory capacity and has been refined by Cowan (2001), who proposes a 4-item limit. For text processing specifically, Daneman and Carpenter (1980) on reading spans provides the more direct connection: readers process text in clause-sized units that map to working memory capacity. Each sense-line should correspond to one such processing unit. (Note: Miller's original "7 ± 2" concerns discrete memory items, not text segmentation directly. The bridge from memory capacity to reading-unit size runs through the reading-span literature, not through Miller alone.)

**Kintsch's propositions** (1998): Comprehension involves constructing a propositional textbase where each proposition is a predicate + arguments. Our "predication test" (the default operationalization of criterion 1) is informed by Kintsch's model — each line should correspond to roughly one Kintsch-level proposition or a small, coherent cluster.

### Why Our Approach Differs from Marschall's Rhetorical-Treatise Grounding

Marschall grounds her colometric criteria in what ancient rhetoricians (Aristotle, Pseudo-Demetrius, Cicero, Quintilian) considered well-formed cola and periods. This is historically appropriate for analyzing texts produced within that rhetorical tradition. It is less directly applicable to the BofM for two reasons:

1. The BofM was not composed within the Graeco-Roman rhetorical tradition (whether its source is ancient Hebrew-influenced or 19th-century American).
2. Our purpose is cognitive readability, not rhetorical analysis.

The psycholinguistic literature (Chafe, Cowan, Kintsch) provides post-hoc grounding that is language-universal and purpose-aligned: it describes how any reader processes text, not how a trained rhetorician would evaluate it. But this grounding explains the methodology; it did not generate it.

### Thought-Marking vs. Structural Syntax

**Thought-marking syntax** reveals where one atomic unit ends and the next begins: main-verb shifts, clause boundaries, subject changes, appositive coreference, camera-angle turns. When a thought-marking feature is present, the author has given evidence that a new atomic unit is beginning. Rules that manipulate thought-marking syntax are making editorial claims about where thought boundaries lie.

**Structural syntax** consists of fixed English/archaic-English patterns that do NOT automatically map to thought boundaries: conditionals, correlatives, comparatives, the AICTP formula, vocatives, fixed idioms. A line break inside a structural pattern is not reading a boundary off the text — it is just following grammar.

The defense against "you are just breaking at every grammatical feature" is: we break at features that mark thought boundaries, not at features that are merely fixed grammatical constructions.

### The "That"-Clause Taxonomy (Complement-Integrity Framework)

Revised 2026-04-17 after corpus sweep (commit `b04cae9`). The original "optional vs. obligatory" framing (from Quirk et al. 1985, section 16.31-35) was too narrow — it treated speech verbs as taking "optional" complements and therefore breaking by default, which produced inconsistent editorial results (a 50/50 corpus split was measured pre-sweep). The corrected principle: **when the "that" clause functions as the verb's semantic complement — i.e., the verb's predication is incomplete without it — the two stay on one line regardless of whether the complement is strictly syntactically required.** This is Rule 17 (Complement Integrity) generalized.

### Semantic Grouping Principle for Compound Lists

Breaks in compound lists should fall at semantic domain boundaries, not arbitrarily. Items sharing a closer semantic relationship stay on the same line.

**Recognized semantic pairings:** gold+silver, copper+brass, iron+steel, flocks+herds, swords+cimeters, famine+pestilence, women+children, statutes+judgments+commandments, murder+plunder+steal, whoredoms+abominations, power+wisdom+understanding.

### Register-Sensitivity Refinement: Gorgianic Pairs vs. Stab-Commata Enumerations

**Added 2026-04-18 (ported from GNT project — Marschall 2 Cor validation 2026-04-13, commit `ff3a4b6`).**

The Semantic Grouping Principle above applies the default compound-list merge logic uniformly. This refinement adds a **register test** that modulates the default in passionate-enumerative contexts. The grammatical test (coordinate items under one governing head) is unchanged; the register test is new.

**The distinction:**

- **Gorgianic pairs (MERGE):** Calm balanced pairing. Coordinate items under one governing head where the items have **no independent rhetorical weight** — they form a unified hendiadys or balanced doublet. Example: *κόπῳ καὶ μόχθῳ* (labor and toil), *statutes and judgments*, *power and wisdom*. The two items together paint one image.

- **Stab-commata enumerations (STACK):** Passionate-enumerative register. Coordinate items where **each item carries its own rhetorical stab** — the cumulative rhetorical effect IS the point. Examples from Paul's peristasis catalog (2 Cor 11:23-27): *ἐν κόποις... ἐν φυλακαῖς... ἐν πληγαῖς... ἐν θανάτοις* (in labors... in imprisonments... in stripes... in deaths) — each clause is its own rhetorical blow. BofM equivalents: Abinadi's indictment chains, Alma's interrogative sermon rhetorical-question chains, Nephi's psalm parallelisms.

**The register test (cognitive prong of "unless" case #1 applied to passionate enumeration):**
- Does each coordinate item carry its own information focus (Chafe idea unit)? If yes, each is its own beat.
- Does the passage's rhetorical register signal passionate enumeration — interrogative sermon, prophetic indictment, liturgical enumeration, Psalm-like anaphora, martial casualty roll, serial woe-formulas?
- If both prongs pass, the items STACK (unless case #1, formally-marked parallel series).

**Candidate BofM registers where the refinement applies:**
- Sermonic interrogative chains (Alma 5 — Alma's "have ye" chain; ~50 rhetorical questions)
- Faith-sermon enumerations (Alma 32 — "whosoever" chains)
- Psalm-like parallelisms (2 Nephi 4 — Nephi's psalm)
- Martial casualty rolls (Mormon 6 — already stacked corpus-wide; worth confirming)
- Prophetic indictment chains (Mosiah 11–17 — Abinadi's serial indictments)
- Serial woe formulas (Helaman 13, 3 Nephi 9)

**Cross-language significance:** this refinement is load-bearing. The original trigger was GNT Marschall's published rhetorical-treatise method converging with session-10's audit-guided Goldilocks method on Paul's peristasis catalog. Two independent methodologies, same result: stab-commata enumerations stack. Cross-language convergence evidence.

**WHY / HOW / SCOPE:**
- **WHY:** Rhetorical register modulates colometric treatment of coordinate items. Passionate-enumerative register means each item has independent rhetorical function; merging flattens the effect.
- **HOW we know:** Convergence between Marschall's published rhetorical-treatise method (grounded in classical canon — Demetrius, Cicero, Quintilian) and session-10 audit-guided Goldilocks method (grounded in syntactic trees). Two independent lineages, same result on Greek corpus. BofM analog audit pending.
- **SCOPE:** Applies to both Greek and English rhetorical prose. Especially relevant in sermonic / interrogative / indictment / liturgical registers. Does NOT override the gorgianic-pair merge rule for calm balanced pairings — the register test is a MODULATION of the default, not a replacement.

### "Insomuch That" — Degree vs. Result

Of 178 corpus instances, 91 are result clauses (correctly split) and 44 are degree modifiers (merged in April 2026 session).

**DEGREE (merge):** The "insomuch that" clause restates, quantifies, or measures the SAME action on the preceding line. No new image is introduced. Three subtypes:
- (a) Same verb/synonym restated: "there arose a division / insomuch that they divided hither and thither" — "divided" IS the "division"
- (b) Quantification: "did slay them with much slaughter / insomuch that there were slain twelve thousand" — adds a number to the same action
- (c) Abstract to specific detail (same image): "it began to be exceedingly difficult / insomuch that we could obtain no food"

**RESULT (break):** The "insomuch that" clause introduces a genuinely new event, actor, or image. Three triggers:
- (d) New actor: "they preached / insomuch that the Lord did bless them"
- (e) New type of action: "their anger did increase / insomuch that they did seek to take away my life"
- (f) New image: "they were astonished / insomuch that they fell to the earth"

**Key patterns:**
- Emotion to same emotion restated: MERGE
- Emotion to physical action: BREAK
- Abstract state to concrete specification: MERGE
- Abstract state to concrete new scene: BREAK
- Same military action measured: MERGE
- Military action to different outcome: BREAK

### Date/Colophon Formula Rule

Editorial timestamps are bureaucratic formulas, not rhetoric. They always stay on one line. 34 merges applied across Alma and Helaman.

### "I Say Unto You" Treatment

**Settled 2026-04-17:** "I say unto you" + indirect-discourse "that" complement is governed by Rule 17 (Complement Integrity). The speech-verb + indirect "that" clause forms one integrated thought and merges by default. Exceptions per Rule 17's exception list (direct discourse with colon/voice shift, AICTP, purpose "that," parallel "that" series → frame+first, meta-announcement, divine recitativum).

The prior framing as "length-dependent editorial judgment" (earlier audit found a 50/50 split across 273 instances) is superseded. The 50/50 split reflected **inconsistent application over multiple editorial sessions before the governing principle was articulated**, not a length-based rule. A 2026-04-17 corpus sweep applied Rule 17 consistently, producing 38 merges across 8 books (commit `b04cae9`).

**Length is a sanity check, not a governing criterion.** Long indirect-discourse lines (e.g., Alma 10:27 at 29 words) merge when the thought is atomic regardless of length. Conversely, short lines do not merge when the "that" clause is appositive (meta-announcement) or parallel (series) rather than complement.

### Verb + "That" Audit Results

**Original audit (2026-03):** A corpus-wide audit of 131 verb + "that" instances tested and rejected the hypothesis that declarative verbs should break while perceptive verbs merge. **Revised finding (2026-04-17 re-audit):** The governing principle is **complement integrity (Rule 17)**, not verb type or length. When a "that" clause is the semantic complement of a governing verb — whether causative, aspectual, speech (indirect discourse), or FEF — the verb and its complement merge. The 2026-03 audit's null result reflected an attempted binary classification (declarative vs. perceptive) that didn't capture the operative distinction. The operative distinction is **complement vs. non-complement "that"-clause**, which cross-cuts traditional verb-class taxonomy.

### "Which" Relative Clauses — Unified Decision Tree

Revised 2026-04-18 after corpus sweep (commit pending this session). The previous "identifier vs. new information" working distinction remains correct but was only one of three rules that bear on "which"-clause placement. The unified decision tree makes all three rules explicit and resolves the apparent inconsistencies as editorial drift rather than rule gaps.

**Three rules governing "which" clauses:**

**Rule α — Class P (Completing-Predication Relative):** When a head noun (or demonstrative pronoun) + restrictive relative are on consecutive lines, and NEITHER line stands alone semantically, MERGE. Diagnostic: can the head-line stand alone without the relative?

**Rule β — Identifier vs. New Information (Working Distinction):** Restrictive relatives that identify/classify their head MERGE. Non-restrictive relatives that predicate new information or a new action/beat EARN their own line.

**Rule γ — Syntactic Affirmation Test:** Non-restrictive relatives with their own subject+verb and complete predication DO have syntactic affirmation for a break. Restrictive relatives tightly bound to their head do NOT.

When all three rules converge, follow. When they appear to conflict, the construction is likely ambiguous and the call is Cat B editorial judgment (prefer the reading that best serves the reader's thought-stream).

**Pattern taxonomy:**

| Pattern | Treatment | Rule(s) that govern | Anchor example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Predicative identifier ("which is X" where X classifies/names) | MERGE | α + β | "lake of fire and brimstone which is the second death" (Jacob 2026-04-18) |

### Class P: Completing-Predication Relative Clauses

A head noun on one line with its completing restrictive relative clause on the next line. The relative fails the atomic-thought test not because it lacks a verb, but because it cannot stand alone and neither can the head.

## Section 9: Superseded Formulations

### The Mar 18 Two-Prong Formulation (first hierarchy)

**Date:** 2026-03-18.

**Formulation:** "Each line must be an atomic thought, an atomic breath unit, or ideally both." When they conflict, editorial judgment decides.

**Why it changed:** This treated thought and breath as co-equal. The Mar 29 and Apr 11 updates established that thought is primary and breath is secondary. The "or" was replaced by a hierarchy.

### The Mar 29 Three-Tier Hierarchy

**Date:** 2026-03-29.

**Formulation:**
1. Syntax (atomic thought) — always governs unless overridden
2. Prosody (atomic breath) — can override syntax only when break is clear and significant
3. Editorial instinct — flags candidates but does not decide

**Why it changed:** This put syntax first as the primary criterion. The Apr 11 harmonization with the GNT project reframed syntax as a servant to thought, not the primary criterion. The two positions are operationally identical but conceptually different — the new framing is more honest about WHY we enforce syntactic bonds (because breaking them would fail the atomic thought test, not because syntax is inherently primary).

### The Apr 11 Syntax-as-Floor Four-Criteria Formulation

**Date:** 2026-04-11.

**Formulation:**
1. English syntax / clause rules (FLOOR — never violate)
2. Atomic idea (PRIMARY criterion)
3. New mental image (STRONG signal)
4. Natural breath pause (SECONDARY — tiebreaker)

**Why it changed:** Same day, the harmonization with the GNT project moved syntax from position 1 to position 4 (servant). Both produce the same edits in every tested case, but the harmonized formulation better reflects the theoretical commitment: atomic thought is the criterion, syntax is the tool that finds it.

### The "Wins Every Collision" Absolutist Version

**Date:** 2026-04-12.

**Formulation:** "Atomic thought wins every collision" — applied without the "unless" mechanism. Predication was implicitly equated with atomic thought.

**Why it changed:** The 2026-04-16 reframe recognized that Chafe's "idea unit" (the claimed theoretical foundation) is broader than predication. The predication test was catching formally-marked parallel series, portrait-building accumulations, speech-act announcements, and classical commata as violations even though they are demonstrable idea units. The "default + unless" mechanism resolved the gap between the claimed foundation and the operational test.
